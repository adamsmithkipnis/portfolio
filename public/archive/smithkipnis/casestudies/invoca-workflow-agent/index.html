Invoca Workflow Agent

A self-initiated, in-platform AI agent for Invoca users that knows who you are, where you are, what you're looking at, and how Invoca works.

Context

Invoca is a conversation intelligence platform. Marketers use it to attribute the ads and web pages that drive inbound phone calls; it routes those calls, handles them with SMS and voice AI agents, and analyzes what was said. It serves Fortune 1000 enterprises, including healthcare institutions, and is used daily by performance marketers, agencies, and customer success managers for monitoring and reporting.

  • The platform: Conversation intelligence for Fortune 1000 enterprises: call attribution, routing, and AI agents.
  • The product: Powerful but non-obvious. A deep but fragmented feature set, with learning resources spread across several separately managed sites, weak search, confusing navigation, and a UI that changed often and carried inconsistencies.
  • The users: Performance marketers and agencies, customer success managers and account managers, and customers in regulated industries, including healthcare.

The constraints: no mandate, no research budget. I started building this during our quarterly passion week because the experience was a vision I believed in, and still believe in.

Discovery

My discovery process was scrappy but real, and it came from three places.

Lunch interviews

Invoca is a remote-first company, so every quarter I met up with a few customer success managers and account managers based in Seattle to watch our all-hands broadcast, then we got lunch. During those lunches I interviewed them about the problems and opportunities they saw, which gave me real insight into the challenges our users were facing and why.

Call mining

I created a Gumloop agent to pull about two years of customer conversation transcripts from Gong into NotebookLM, and used that to build a tool where my colleagues could ask things like "What are the top 5 usability issues?" and get answers with citations to specific customer quotes. Product and UX colleagues adopted it.

Decision guides

Early in the year I was given a side project to build a decision guide that explained Invoca's capabilities and recommended the right ones by use case. Honestly, that project felt like busywork. But the documentation I created for it, mapping every Invoca capability to specific use cases and reasoning, became part of the backbone of the agent's knowledge base.

What customers said

The top issues across roughly two years of customer calls, surfaced by the NotebookLM project with citations back to specific calls and timestamps:

"Can't drill down into dashboards."

"Unintuitive keyword search."

"Frequent UI changes make features hard to find."

All of these point at the same thing: people couldn't find what they wanted inside the product, so they went somewhere else for it. This was real customer language at scale, with no research budget. And the pattern across all three inputs was the big-ticket item: users, including Invoca employees, were already using AI, just not inside Invoca.

Problem

That tab is a leak

Invoca's users wanted to analyze call transcripts with AI, but there was too much friction, so they opened a new tab, copied transcripts out of Invoca, and pasted them into ChatGPT or Gemini, because that was a better experience. Lean manufacturing would call this transport waste, but it's more serious than that: some of our customers were healthcare institutions, so pasting caller transcripts into consumer AI tools risked leaking PHI and PII. That was not how conversation data was supposed to be handled.

The workflow as it stood: transcripts copied out of Invoca and pasted into a consumer AI tool

I didn't see this as users being malicious; I saw rational behavior driven by incentives and an inefficient UI. And because all of those analytics queries were happening outside Invoca, we had zero telemetry on what users were actually trying to do. I chose to fix the incentive I could fix, by making a better experience inside the platform, one that would give us telemetry as a byproduct.

Three problems worth solving

  • Unintuitive platform UI: Learning resources were scattered across several sites, so customers leaned on customer success managers to implement solutions for them, unbilled, which was expensive for Invoca.
  • Data leakage: Transcripts were leaving the platform for ChatGPT and Gemini, a privacy and compliance risk.
  • No telemetry: The platform had no view into user intent. An in-platform agent's chat logs would be live research.

Those were the three that mattered most, not the only three. An MCP server letting third-party agents act inside Invoca would arguably have been a better user experience, and I did bring it up, but it was too heavy a lift in the time available and worked against the leakage problem without appropriate guardrails, so it went onto the long-term vision roadmap instead.

My role

Nobody assigned this to me. I'd been thinking about the concept for months and started building it during passion week, because a working demo would move the conversation further than a deck would. I wore several hats, and built and deployed the result to the internal QA platform behind a feature flag.

Interaction model

One agent, many forms

My core design principle was Omnipresence. The agent is always there, on every page, and can be called up from wherever you are. It is not a destination you navigate to. That produced three states: a persistent pill in the platform chrome, built onto Invoca's frontend shell so it rides along on every route; a popover that floats over the current page for a quick question; and an embedded drawer that docks beside the content for longer sessions.

The trade-off between the last two is the page underneath. A panel that overlays the dashboard makes the agent useless at the task it's best at, which is explaining what's on screen. So the embedded state reflows the dashboard grid into the narrower space rather than covering it, at the cost of a heavier, slower transition. The popover deliberately leaves the page untouched and accepts the occlusion. I kept both instead of choosing, and made it something the user can adjust to their preference, because the question being asked determines which cost is acceptable.

The agent

The Workflow Agent running on the platform, on the Manage Campaigns page with demo data

This is the real agent, labeled "Invoca Assistant" in the product. Here it answers what the Payout column means by reading the table on screen. In the demo, a new user also asked for step-by-step instructions on creating and activating a campaign, and the answer referenced the actual buttons on the page. Context engineering

An agent's usefulness is a function of its context, so deciding what it should know is a set of design decisions about what users will ask and what a good answer requires. I specified four pillars of context.

  • Who I am: The agent knows who you are: name, role, and organization type are passed in, so responses are personalized and permission-aware. A Network user and a Publisher user asking the same question are not asking the same question.
  • Where I am: It knows where you are: it receives the current URL, and on top of that, I built a navigation map of the site, its routes, views and structures. Without it, an agent can tell you that a setting exists; with it, it can tell you where to click.
  • What I see: It knows what you are looking at by scraping the current page. Asking what the payout column means only works when the agent can see the payout column, so page reading came first.
  • How Invoca works: It knows what Invoca knows by retrieving contextualized information from our documentation, with citations, so it doesn't guess, and accuracy is easily verified.

All four assemble into one context per request. Every stream was sourced, formatted, and tested on its own, and extending the context makes the answers better. Four pillars because four was what we could deliver, not because the model stops there. The roadmap carried further pillars as the north star.

Knowledge base

Retrieval is only as good as its corpus, so I built the corpus myself. Invoca's documentation was scattered across several knowledge bases, each managed by a different group with its own interface, and none of them were integrated into the platform in a way that gave users contextual answers. I crawled every learning resource that existed: the Invoca Academy course library, transcripts of training videos, developer documentation, platform docs, and the full support center knowledge base, which contains several hundred articles, converting everything to Markdown. I wrote the navigation map from the platform's own route structure. And the decision guides from discovery became a technology-selector corpus, covering the choices our support team fields constantly and that our docs only address indirectly: Semantic Signal versus Rule-Based Signal, Lookup Table versus Formula Field.

I formatted all of the documents so a retriever could use them reliably. On top of the raw corpus, I built a synthesized wiki with concept pages, source summaries, and a maintained index, prototyped in Obsidian as a side project and drawing on Andrej Karpathy's "personal knowledge base" architecture. The agent itself we assembled in Polaris as a sequential agent graph: a state object and conversation memory feeding a start node, an agent node with a retriever tool over the document vector store, and an end node.

Chunk size was the setting I spent longest on, because every wrong value failed differently. Too small and answers came back incomplete or lost their citations; too large and they turned redundant, drifted from the source, or landed in the uncanny valley, technically responsive and still wrong to read.

In the screens below, a red outline marks what is in the agent's context and a red tint what the agent can read. On the roadmap further down, a blue tint marks what it can read and write.

Stage 1, Know Invoca. A ubiquitous Q&A agent, preloaded with Q&A pairs and documents via RAG, so people onboard faster and get just-in-time answers without searching for them. Click and ask. Example: "How do I search for a call record by a caller's phone number?"

Stage 2, Who and where am I? The agent infers context from the URL and the user. Because it knows which page a user is on, and who the user is, it personalizes answers, addresses users by name, and offers quick-reply shortcuts for the questions that page tends to raise. A map of the entire platform, its routes, views and structure, guides users to the right place.

Stage 3, See what I see. The agent knows the page content, the user's location on the platform, navigation paths, permissions, user ID, and Invoca's product documentation. Responses are grounded in what's on screen.

Stage 4, See everything. On-demand read access to everything the user already has access to, so they can ask about their data and campaigns from any page, and the agent can pull that data from across the platform. Example: from the call transcript page, ask about average sales conversion rates in Santa Barbara without leaving the page.

Stage 5, Act locally. Taking inspiration from Braintrust's Loop, the agent gains read and write actions on the page the user is on, using natural language to orchestrate them. Page-based actions can also navigate the user elsewhere. Example: on the reports page, "show me all call records from (555) 867-5309."

Stage 6, Act globally. Actions on data and functions that cross multiple pages, read and write, remote, plus navigation. Example: "create an SMS agent for me," using an agent to create an agent.

Stage 7, Collaborate. Users connect their own external services and CRM systems for orchestration beyond the platform: Salesforce, HubSpot, and other systems' MCP servers.

Trust & buy-in

Observability

Every interaction was logged to Braintrust: the system prompt, the assembled user context, the scraped page content, and the conversation. When anyone asked how the agent knew something, the answer included a link to one of our published knowledge bases. During our live demo, we refreshed Braintrust in real time and read the trace of the conversation we had just had.

Winning over the docs teams

There was real anxiety in the organization that a retrieval agent would devalue the support and Academy teams' work. I made the opposite argument publicly, because the project would have failed politically otherwise. Content from support.invoca.com and the Academy doesn't become less valuable because an agent can retrieve it. It gets a new channel. What is changing is that agents are becoming a bigger consumer of documentation than humans are, which argues for investing in documentation.

I got that point about agents consuming documentation from Hahnbee Lee, a founder of Mintlify, at Braintrust's 2026 Trace conference in San Francisco, where she said close to 50% of their usage was already from agents and was trending toward 90%.

Challenges

Build versus buy

The honest challenge was build versus buy. Invoca was already close to committing to ThoughtSpot's AI agents, which were specific to data dashboards, and the Workflow Agent was treated as a longer-term initiative. Even though it was fully built and deployed to QA behind a feature flag, with employees using it extensively, it didn't launch to customers while I was there. The organization chose a different path.

The response

My response had two parts. First, I kept making the case for native context: an external tool can't know the user's current page, role, or platform structure the way an in-platform agent can. Second, when I was asked to show how ThoughtSpot's AI agents could be implemented inside Invoca, I did it quickly, as a Figma Make prototype and a video walkthrough, carrying over what I'd learned about context. The concept and framing shaped how the organization approached in-platform AI from there.

Impact

  • Over 100 unique sessions per day: once we rolled it out to colleagues on the internal QA platform, usage climbed immediately, passed 100 unique sessions a day, outperformed Invoca's shipped SMS and Voice Agents, and grew every day it ran.
  • Three days, one engineer: Gabriel and I built the majority of it in about three days, deployed it to QA behind a feature flag, and it was one of three passion projects selected for live presentation to the company.
  • Working proof of concept: a live demonstration of page awareness, URL awareness, user awareness, cited retrieval from Invoca's documentation, and conversation persistence across navigation, on real platform screens.
  • Adjacent impact: the NotebookLM insights tool was adopted by Product and UX, and the work earned a peer-nominated Impact Award.
  • Roadmap influence: the concept and its framing shaped how Invoca approached in-platform AI afterward. The company has since put AI agents at the center of its platform strategy, and Nico, the agent it launched in June 2026, carried the framing forward.
  • The open problem: a data-access problem rather than a design one. The use case that started the project, analyzing a call transcript in place, needed transcript data that staging did not have. So we proved page reading separately, then generated simulated transcripts in the JSON format a user would have, using a prompt from my colleague Tristan Marshall, and analyzed them by pasting them into the agent's chat window. The original workflow still required a copy and paste; a live deployment, with real data, is what closes it.

Learnings

  • Scrappy is real: lunch interviews plus mining two years of calls gave me a clearer picture than waiting for a research budget would have.
  • Build the argument: a working agent with real usage moved the organization further than any proposal deck.
  • Context is the product: models are increasingly interchangeable; what makes an assistant useful is knowing the user, the page, and the platform.

Native context beats bolt-on AI.
